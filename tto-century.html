Starts have been shrinking for a century. One number refused to move.

After our last piece on the third time through the order, one question kept coming back. Cardinals starters rarely finish the sixth inning these days. How unusual is this? Hitters do better the third time they see a starter — the third-time penalty — and managers pull pitchers to dodge it. Every early hook you've groaned at is a bet on this penalty so it had better be real. We went to the record to check — every major-league start from 1910 through 2025, rebuilt play by play.

Here is the finding. Everyone says pitchers get pulled earlier than ever. By the third-trip measure, they are pulled about as often as they were sixty years ago. From 1950 through 1969, 84% of starts reached a third trip. Over the past decade, 84%. (Finishing the trip is another matter — the fourth-lap numbers below cover it.) What changed is why the other one start in six ends early. Then, an early exit meant the pitcher was getting hit. Now it is usually the plan. Same rate, different reason — that is the story of this piece.

The full century says the same. Starts have collapsed by every measure of length: innings, batters faced, full trips through the order. Yet the share of starters who face a lineup a third time has barely moved. It was 88% in 1910. It was 85% in 2025. What has happened is that the hook has marched backward one lap at a time. The fifth trip through the order is gone. The last one belongs to David Price, who faced 37 batters for Detroit on September 6, 2014. The fourth trip is nearly gone. The third trip is where the retreat stands now. Starters still meet a lineup a third time. They just no longer finish the lap.

If starters no longer faced lineups a third time, the top line would fall with the others. It doesn't: 88% of starts in 1910, 85% in 2025. The two lines below it collapsed.

In 1910, three starts in four included a fourth trip through the order (1,850 of 2,456). One in four included a fifth. Pitchers who started games finished them, however long they ran. Dizzy Dean's 1934 season shows the old normal: 33 starts, 30 fourth trips, 12 fifth trips. In 2025 the entire league, across 4,859 starts, took 238 fourth trips and no fifth trips. Dean took more fifth trips by himself in 1934 than all thirty teams have taken since 2015.

A note on those old totals. A fifth trip usually meant a long game or extra innings, and the starter simply stayed for all of it. That was the era's custom. The man who started the game was expected to end it, however long it took.

The leaders tell the same story in one line. Robin Roberts took 38 fourth trips in 1953 (41 starts). Wilbur Wood took 38 in 1972 (49 starts). David Price led baseball with 28 in 2014 (34 starts). In 2024 the leader, Max Fried, took eight (29 starts). In 2025 it was Framber Valdez, with ten (31 starts). Even the workhorses have given up the fourth lap.

Through all of that, the top line barely bent. A third trip begins with the 19th batter of a game. The typical 2025 start ran 23 batters, four past the start of a third trip.

Both familiar measures fell for a hundred years. Neither one says who faces the lineup a third time — that is the top line in chart 1, and it held its level while these collapsed.

The trends match what fans believe about how the game has changed. The typical start lost 10 batters and more than two innings across the century. But the third-lap line moved on its own schedule.

The 1950s dip came from wild swings in how long outings ran. The same 1953 league that let Robin Roberts finish nearly everything also knocked one starter in six out before the 19th batter. Managers were not planning short starts; hitters were forcing them.

The peak came half a century later, and it is recent enough to feel strange. In 2011 and 2014, 95 of every 100 starts reached a third trip. Pitch counts had made outings uniform. Nearly every healthy starter got his six innings, and few got more. Chris Carpenter's Cy Young season in 2005 drew the era's ceiling: 23 fourth trips (33 starts), tied for second in baseball, and not one fifth. Adam Wainwright's 2014 fits the same mold: 19 fourth trips (32 starts), tied for fourth in all of baseball. Robin Roberts alone had doubled that figure in 1953.

The fall is the newest part. From 2015 to 2019, the third-lap share dropped from 92% to 84%. This time hitters were not forcing it. Teams began ending starts mid-lap on purpose. The two lowest shares ever came right after: 70% in the 60-game 2020 season, then 78% in 2021. The number has since settled near 85%. The result is that every modern third-pass number comes only from starts managers chose to extend.

If early exits still meant shelled pitchers, this line would stay high. It has fallen by three quarters: the early-exit second pass is now .21 of OPS worse than the survivors' (3,977 against 37,297 plate appearances in 2025).

A short start also changed its meaning. In the 1950s, hitters' OPS on the second pass in starts that ended early ran about .850 higher than in starts that continued. An early exit meant the pitcher was getting hit. In 2025 the difference was .212. More and more, teams plan the short start before the game begins.

Baseball even ran the experiment for us. From 1973 through 2021, the American League had a designated hitter and the National League did not. NL managers had a second, non-pitching reason to end a start: pinch-hitting for the pitcher. It shows exactly where it should. The two leagues reached the third trip at nearly the same rate in every era. But during the split years, an AL early exit stayed a sign of trouble, while the NL's told less. The NL's early-exit gap ran 60 to 90 points of OPS lower. When the NL adopted the DH in 2022, the two leagues snapped back together within two dozen points. A rule change moved this line on schedule, twice. That is how we know it measures the reason for the hook, not something else.

The early-exit gap by league. The leagues matched before 1973, split while only the AL had the DH (the NL's extra pinch-hit exits made its early exits less about being hit), and rejoined within two seasons of the NL adopting it in 2022.

You can see the same change on the 2025 Cardinals: 8 fourth trips all season, four of them by Sonny Gray (32 starts, in his final St. Louis season). And Matthew Liberatore — the pitcher our last piece centered on — reached a third trip in 26 of his 29 starts in 2025. He reached a fourth in none. His third-pass record exists entirely inside the modern leash.

If earlier hooks shrank the measured penalty, seasons on the left would sit low. The 1950s and 1960s, when the fewest starts reached a third trip, sit high. The 2005–2014 seasons, which allowed the most, sit mid-range. No slope, no fingerprint.

Our last piece measured the penalty at about 30 points of OPS in 2026 — the same starts, third trip against second. That number has a built-in problem, and we said so at the time. It comes only from starts a manager allowed to continue. Hook earlier, and the surviving third trips come from the better days. The measured penalty could shrink without a single pitcher changing. Testing whether the hook bends the penalty took a century of data.

The test came back empty, and the numbers say it plainly. From 1950 to 1969, managers hooked earliest: 84% of starts reached a third trip, and the measured penalty averaged 96 points — the century's largest. From 2005 to 2014, managers were at their most patient: 94% reached, and the penalty averaged 59 points — among the century's smallest. If hooks were shrinking the penalty, those pairs should run the other way. The year-to-year record agrees: across 116 seasons, a season's change in third-trip share says nothing about its change in penalty. Team by team since the league reached 30 clubs in 1998 — 840 team-seasons — there is no link either. The lesson: the penalty we print measures pitchers, not manager patience.

So, back to the question we started with: why care about the third time through when starters barely finish the sixth? Because the third trip is the only deep lap left, and the penalty is the reason. When you see a third-pass number, read it as a pair: the penalty, and how many starts managers let get there. In 2026 so far, that pair is 30 points at 84%. The first number is real — a century of data says the hook never bent it. The second is the one a hundred years of baseball kept quietly changing.

How we measured it

Retrosheet play-by-play for every regular-season game, 1910 through 2025, using only games with complete accounts. Coverage is the record's, not ours: 69% of games in the worst year (1944), 100% every season since 1969. Definitions match our earlier pieces — the starter, each batter's pass count against him, and OPS built from the raw events. "Reached a lap" means the starter faced at least one batter that many times. In seasons without a designated hitter, the batters a starter faces include the opposing pitcher; that lowers every cell's OPS but mostly cancels in the differences we quote. League splits classify each start by the pitching team's league. As a check, the 2025 season computed this way matches our published 2025 baseline exactly. The charts draw from the same tables the sentences quote.
