[Ted to write headlines]

A double play tells you who was batting. The gap between the two answers tells you where the infield stood.

On September 5 we reported that 20 of the Cardinals’ 57 keystone double plays in 2026 had started at shortstop, against a league norm of 55%. The count was right. Through the games of September 6 it stands at 20 of 58, the lowest share of the 30 teams. The reason is the hitters the Cardinals faced, and the infield had little to do with it.

A ground ball that becomes a 6-4-3 was almost always hit by a right-handed hitter, and a 4-6-3 was almost always hit by a lefty. In 2026, 77% of right-handed hitters’ keystone double plays have started at shortstop. For left-handed hitters the figure is 5%. A righty pulls the ball toward the shortstop. A lefty pulls it toward the second baseman. The infield turns whatever comes.

So the first question about any team’s split is who it faced. Left-handed hitters have hit into 45% of the Cardinals’ keystone double plays in 2026, against 38% for the league. That is the third most lefty-heavy mix in baseball. The pitching staff is part of that. Left-handers threw 21% of the Cardinals’ keystone double plays in 2026, against 32% for the league, and lefty pitchers see more right-handed bats. Given the hitters they faced, the Cardinals should have turned about 26 of their 58 at shortstop. They turned 20. A team with their mix falls that far short about 1 in 13 times. That is unusual and short of an outlier. Texas, at 15 of 40, sits at the same odds in 2026.

What remains after the adjustment is small. Against right-handed hitters the Cardinals have turned 20 of 32 at shortstop in 2026. That is 63% against the league’s 77%, and 32 plays are too few to call. Against left-handed hitters they have turned 0 of 26 at shortstop. The league rate is 5%. Masyn Winn and JJ Wetherholt were the pair on 45 of the 58 in 2026. The pattern belongs to one infield, and that infield is doing what every infield does.

The record says the hitter has always decided. Stan Musial hit into 118 keystone double plays that went 4-6-3 from 1942 to 1963, and 29 that went 6-4-3. Albert Pujols ran it the other way from 2001 to 2022: 173 at shortstop, 34 at second. Carl Yastrzemski split 23 and 180 from 1961 to 1983. Hank Aaron split 168 and 40 from 1954 to 1976. Wade Boggs, the classic opposite-field hitter, went 81 and 71 from 1982 to 1999. He is the only left-handed hitter with 120 or more keystone double plays whose plays started at shortstop more often than at second. The 2026 leaders read the same way. Bryce Harper has hit into 12 keystone double plays in 2026 and all 12 went 4-6-3. Corey Seager: 10 of 10 in 2026. Alec Bohm and Manny Machado, both right-handed, have sent 9 of 10 apiece to the shortstop in 2026. Among Cardinals in 2026, Alec Burleson is 0 of 6 at shortstop and Iván Herrera is 6 of 6.

Take every team-season since 2000 with at least 20 keystone double plays, 763 of them, and the shortstop share spreads .074 from team to team. Adjust each team for the hitters it faced that season and the spread falls to .060. A coin flipped that many times per team would spread .063. That leaves the infield nothing to explain. We checked the fielders and the managers anyway. When a team brought back the same shortstop and second baseman, its share correlated .176 with the year before. When it changed at least one of them, .172. Across 106 managers with eight or more seasons, career averages spread .021, and noise alone would spread them .018. Neither the fielders nor the managers carry the split.

How far apart the two hitters sit

The gap between the right-handed and left-handed 6-4-3 rates, by decade and then by season around the 2023 shift ban. The widest gap before the ban was .66, in 2021. The four seasons since are the four widest in the record.

The gap is the part of this that changes, and it changes with where infielders stand. It was .24 in the 1910s, when a shortstop played a lefty about where he played a righty. It reached .58 in the 1960s, loosened to .43 in the 1990s, and climbed back to .53 in the 2010s. The three seasons before the ban averaged .64. The years when the gap loosened line up with artificial turf and its retreat. We have not tested that, and the methods section says so.

The pooled 55% hid all of this for a century. From the 1920s through the 2010s the pooled share stayed between 53% and 57% by decade. The left-handed rate, meanwhile, fell from 28% to 16% and rose back to 28%. Lefties hit into 26% to 30% of keystone double plays in every one of those decades, so the average barely moved. The pooled number cannot see positioning. The gap can.

Lefties stopped hitting 6-4-3s

The 6-4-3 share for each side, by season. Right-handed hitters went from 70% in 2010 to 79% in 2023 and held. Left-handed hitters went from 25% in the 2000s to 12% in the four seasons before the ban to 6% in the three seasons after it. The red points are the Cardinals’ 2026 rates: 20 of 32 against righties, 0 of 26 against lefties.

The ban did the opposite of what many expected. The shortstop can no longer stand on the right side of second base. So the second baseman now fields every pulled ground ball from a lefty, and a lefty’s double play goes 4-6-3 almost every time. The ban moved the fielder. The ball still goes to the same place. The pooled share climbed to 61% in 2022 for a different reason: lefties fell to 18% of keystone double plays that year. In 2026 they are 38% of them, the highest share in the record. The league’s pooled share has fallen to 49%, from 54% in 2025. A fixed 55% norm reads that drift as thirty teams changing their infields.

We are rebuilding the check that flagged the Cardinals so that it compares a team to the hitters it faced, in the same season. Until then, read a 6-4-3 count the way the record does: it tells you who was batting. The number that says where the infield stood is the gap between the two hitters. It has sat wider in the four seasons since the ban than in any season since 1910.

HOW WE MEASURED IT

What counts as a keystone double play

We counted ground-ball double plays that the record charges to the batter and that ran shortstop-to-second-to-first (6-4-3) or second-to-shortstop-to-first (4-6-3). Together they are the keystone family. Other double plays, such as 5-4-3 or 3-6-3, stay outside it. The 6-4-3 share is the family’s shortstop-started plays divided by all family plays.

Every play carries the batter’s side: right, left, or switch hitter. Retrosheet records a switch hitter as a switch hitter; it does not say which side he batted from on that play. So switch hitters are their own group in every table here. The two side rates the piece quotes are for right-handed and left-handed hitters only.

Two sources, one definition

Seasons 1910 through 2025 come from Retrosheet play-by-play, regular season only. Play-by-play coverage begins in 1910. Across the 116 seasons, 49 family plays lack a batter side and one ground-ball double play lacks a fielding sequence; those plays are excluded and counted in the coverage table of the verification workbook.

Seasons 2025 and 2026 also come from MLB’s play-by-play feed. A play’s direction comes from the fielding credits on the play, read in order. As a check, we also read it from the play’s written description (”shortstop A to second baseman B to first baseman C”). The 2025 pooled share is .539 from MLB’s feed and .539 from Retrosheet. The side rates differ a little between the two sources because Retrosheet keeps switch hitters as a third group while MLB’s feed records the side each one batted from.

The 2026 window is every completed regular-season game played before September 7, 2026: 2,154 games and 1,371 keystone plays. The credit chain and the description parse agree on 3,067 of 3,194 double plays of every kind; the rest are descriptions the parser does not read, such as a runner thrown out on the bases. The credit chain is the classification the piece uses.

The gap is the right-handed rate minus the left-handed rate. It is the piece’s positioning instrument: the farther apart the two rates sit, the more completely infields are shaded to each hitter’s pull side.

The hand-adjusted spread and the coin-flip floor

For each team-season we computed the 6-4-3 share it would have posted if every hitter it faced had gone the league’s way that season:

expected share = (righties faced x league RHB rate
               +  lefties faced x league LHB rate
               +  switch hitters faced x league switch rate) / family DPs
residual       = actual share - expected share

The league rates are the same season’s rates, so a team is compared at the same season and the same mix of hitters. Team-seasons with fewer than 20 family double plays are left out of every spread.

The coin-flip floor is the spread those team-seasons would show with no team effect at all. Each team-season’s expected share and its number of family plays give the spread a pure coin flip would produce, and the floor is the square root of the average of those.

floor = sqrt( average over team-seasons of  e (1 - e) / n )

For 2000 through 2025, the 763 team-seasons at or above 20 family plays spread .074 in raw share. After the hand adjustment they spread .060. The floor is .063. There is nothing left for the infield to explain.

Reproducing the 55% norm

The September 5 edition compared the Cardinals to a 55% norm across 763 team-seasons since 2000. Rebuilt here from the raw plays, the same window and the same 20-play floor give 763 team-seasons, a mean share of .5525 and a spread of .0740. The detector’s committed snapshot carries .5522 and .0741. The difference is the 49 plays without a batter side.

The Cardinals test

The Cardinals’ 2026 count is tested against the share expected from the hitters they faced, using an exact binomial probability. One-sided: the chance a team with their expected share and their number of family plays would turn that many or fewer at shortstop. Two-sided: the chance of any count at least as unlikely as theirs, in either direction. In 2026 the Cardinals turned 20 of 58 at shortstop against an expected share of .447; the probabilities are .075 one-sided and .146 two-sided. The piece quotes the one-sided figure as “about 1 in N” and the team_2026 tab of the workbook carries both for all 30 teams. This is a different instrument from the delete-one-start confidence bounds our pitching pieces use, because a double-play count is one binomial draw, not a per-start average; the workbook’s team_2026 tab recomputes both probabilities from the counts.

Do the fielders carry it? The same-pair test

For every team with 20 or more family plays in two consecutive seasons, we found the shortstop and second baseman credited on the most family plays in each season. When both men returned, the two seasons’ raw shares correlate .176 (666 season pairs, 1950 through 2025). When at least one changed, .172 (1,177 pairs). The hand-adjusted residuals correlate .136 and .081. A returning pair carries no more of the split from one year to the next than a new one does, and the residual carries almost nothing at all.

Do managers carry it?

Each team-season was assigned the manager who ran the most games. Among 106 managers with 8 or more qualifying team-seasons between 1910 and 2025, career-average residuals spread .021. Sampling noise alone would spread them .018 with the same career lengths. The difference amounts to about one 6-4-3 per hundred family double plays. The extremes, Kevin Cash at +.068 over 11 seasons and Pat Moran at -.062 over 9, are what the tails of 106 noisy averages look like. We did not find a manager effect worth a sentence in the piece.

What we ran and set aside

Three routes did not add to the story. The same-pair test and the manager test above are two of them. The third is the pooled 6-4-3 share itself as an instrument: it has sat near 55% since the 1920s because the lefty share of family plays stayed between .26 and .30 for ten decades while both side rates moved together, so it cannot show what the two rates are doing. The gap can.

An open question we did not chase

The gap loosened from .58 in the 1960s to .43 in the 1990s before climbing again. The years line up with artificial turf and its retreat, but this piece did not test that. It is a hypothesis, named here so no reader takes the chart as an answer to it.
