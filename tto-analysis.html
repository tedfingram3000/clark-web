Three times through, once more with context

A new way we’ll read pitchers — starting with the stat we quote most · 2026-08-20

This week we told you Reds starter Brady Singer was carrying a strange split: hitters had posted a .684 OPS against him the first time through the order, .978 the second time, and .735 the third. Read quickly, that says batters figure him out and then he figures them back. Read carefully, it says something is wrong with how everyone quotes this stat, us included.

Here’s the problem. A pitcher’s first- and second-pass numbers come from essentially every start he makes. His third-pass numbers come only from the games his manager let him keep pitching, and managers mostly do that when the first two passes went well. The third time through isn’t a random sample of the same pitcher. It’s a highlight reel of his better days, graded on days he’d already survived.

Analysts call this survivorship bias, and the researchers who’ve studied times through the order have all had to wrestle with some version of it. Mitchel Lichtman, co-author of The Book, measured the decline at roughly ten points of wOBA per pass across every start from 2000 through 2012 — and had to adjust for the quality of the pitchers and hitters involved precisely because the raw splits wouldn’t hold still. Russell Carleton at Baseball Prospectus found the decline tracks pitch count more than batter familiarity: tired arms, more than hitters solving a repertoire. And in 2022, three academic statisticians (Ryan Brill, Sameer Deshpande, and Abraham Wyner) modeled every plate appearance and concluded that pitchers fade continuously through a game rather than falling off a cliff at any one pass. Three methods, one shared conclusion: the fade through a game is real. The argument is over why it happens and how badly the naive splits measure it — and Singer’s line is a walking exhibit of the measurement problem.

So we rebuilt the stat from the play-by-play, one plate appearance at a time, for every Cardinals start this season — 127 games — plus Singer’s 24 for Cincinnati. Three ways to look at it, each telling you something the season split hides.

First: how often does a third pass even happen? Cardinals starters reached the third time through in 112 of 127 games, 88 percent. Andre Pallante leads the staff at 23 of 24 starts. Singer, for all the noise in his splits, got there in 22 of 24. The hook almost never spares anyone a third pass.

Second: what was the second pass like in the games that produced a third? This is where the aggregate lies. As a staff, Cardinals starters have allowed a .685 OPS the second time through in games where they pitched on into a third pass, and a 1.048 OPS in that same second pass in the games where a third never came (94 plate appearances). Same pitchers, same pass, 363 points apart. The second-pass number you see in a season split averages the days a starter earned a longer leash with the days he was gone by the fifth. Matthew Liberatore is the cleanest example. His season second-pass OPS is .757, but that’s a .648 across his 22 starts that reached a third pass, blended with a 1.745 across the 22 second-pass plate appearances from his three starts that never got there. Those three are what we mean by an early hook: starts where the manager went to the bullpen before any hitter came up a third time — sometimes after a complete second pass, sometimes in the middle of it. Singer is the extreme case: his .978 second pass is .898 in the games he survived — still bad, but a different animal — inflated by a 1.901 across the 17 second-pass plate appearances in his two starts that ended early.

The season number blends two different pitchers: the one who earned third passes and the one who got pulled. 22 PA is a count, not a stat — which is the point. And the amber bar is the other thing the season split hides: in the very starts he entered so well, the third pass cost him 443 points.

Third: within the survival games, what actually happens on the third pass? Compare each pitcher’s third pass to his own second pass in the same games, and the staff gets about 125 points of OPS worse. Fatigue and familiarity doing what they do. For scale: across the full 2025 season, league starters who earned a third pass gave back 49 points on it, a .707 second-pass OPS in those games rising to .756 across 21,685 third-pass plate appearances. This staff’s slide is roughly two and a half times the league’s most recent full-season number. The third pass itself is routine everywhere: 2025 starters reached one in 85 percent of starts.

But the individuals split in opposite directions, and this is the finding that changes how we’ll write. Liberatore, so good the second time through in his survival starts, collapses by 443 points on the third pass; his steady-looking season splits hide the sharpest cliff on the staff. Singer is the mirror image: in the very games that produced his third-pass .735, hitters had just put up .898 the pass before. He’s one of the few starters anywhere who improves the third time through — the one thing his raw splits accidentally got right, for the wrong reason.

The same season split made Singer look scary and Liberatore look steady at exactly the passes where the opposite is true. The stat points backwards.

What changes in 700 Clark. Starting soon, when we cite times-through-the-order numbers you’ll see them with their context attached: how many starts actually produced a third pass, and, where the samples are honest, how the second pass looked in those same games. When a third-pass number rests on fewer than 50 plate appearances, we’ll give you the count instead of a stat that isn’t earned. The pass-by-pass split is honest arithmetic on a lopsided sample — incomplete rather than wrong — and the difference between those two things is most of what this newsletter is for.
