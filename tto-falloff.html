The pitchers who fall off — and the ones who don’t

We rebuilt the times-through-the-order stat for all 3,928 starts in baseball · 2026-08-24 · numbers through 2026-08-23

St. Louis starters give back 107 points of OPS the third time through the order, more than three times what the league gives back, and the stat most fans check can’t see it. That is what came back when we rebuilt times-through-the-order numbers one plate appearance at a time, for every start in the majors this season — 3,928 starts across 1,964 games. The league-wide result is mostly good news for pitchers: measured fairly, the average third-time penalty is about 30 points of OPS, smaller than the raw splits make it look. The Cardinals are the exception. Matthew Liberatore’s fall from his second time through to his third is the fifth-biggest in baseball, and his steady-looking season line hides all of it. Andre Pallante (now on the IL) is the counterweight: 23 third passes in 24 starts, and he holds up under the same test that catches Liberatore.

If you take one thing to the group chat, take this: a starter’s times-through-the-order split blends two different kinds of game — the days he earned a long outing and the days he got pulled early. His third-time line comes only from his good days, while his second-time line mixes both, so the split can make a collapsing pitcher look steady and an improving one look scary. The fair question is what a pitcher’s third pass did compared to his own second pass in the same games. Ask it that way and the dreaded third-time collapse shrinks to 30 points league-wide, while a handful of real cliffs — one of them in St. Louis — stand out plainly.

In the last piece we showed the blend using Cardinals starts alone. This one runs the whole league: who genuinely falls off, why the standard split misses them, and, at the end, the leaderboard we built and refused to publish.

Start with the thing everyone gets wrong. A starter’s second-pass number is an average of two different games. In the starts where he stayed in long enough to face the order a third time, hitters posted a .717 OPS against the league’s second pass. In the starts where he was gone before that, they posted .956. Same pitchers, same pass, 239 points apart. That gap is no 2026 fluke — we measured it at .212 across all of last season too. Managers pull starters who are getting hit in the middle innings, so the second-pass line you see in a season split is a blend of the good days and the disasters, and the third-pass line only ever comes from the good days.

Now the part that surprised us. Once you compare a starter’s third pass to his own second pass in the same games — the only apples-to-apples version of this question — the league gives back 30 points of OPS. That’s it. (The full-2025 figure, which we quoted in the first piece, was 49.) The raw progression everyone quotes (.697, .739, .747) tells a story of steady erosion; the fair test says the third pass costs about 30 points, and in games that were still close, 21. The typical starter’s third time through is a much smaller problem than the standard split makes it look.

That small number survives the obvious objections. Split the season by how many pitches the starter had thrown, and the decline is still there in the range where second and third passes overlap — 67 points, if anything larger, though comparing two passes at the same pitch count quietly swaps one selection problem for another, so read that as survival rather than growth. Split it by score, and blowouts do inflate it: starters left in while losing badly gave back 116 points on the third pass, against at least 21 in games still close. Split it by how good the opposing lineup was, and nothing moves at all — 28, 30 and 32 points against weak, average and strong offenses. Pitch count, score and opponent each get their turn, and the decline outlives all three. The third-time-through penalty is real, it is small, and no control we could put on it made it go away.

A word about how long starters actually last, because most people reading this believe they get pulled early now, and that belief sits right on top of these numbers. In 2026 starters have reached a third time through in 84 percent of starts. The median start is 23 batters faced — about two and a half turns. Whatever has happened to the length of a start, the third time through has not become rare.

Two units are getting mixed together here. “Going deep” usually means innings, and this measures turns through the order, and they come apart: a pitcher who retires 18 in six clean innings never faces the order a third time, while one who labors through 27 in five does. There is also a harder question underneath, which we are looking at next rather than answering here. Managers pull starters partly because they believe in the third-time penalty, and everything we just measured comes from the starts they chose to extend. Hook earlier and the third passes that survive are the best days only, which would make the penalty look smaller than it is. That cuts against our own number, and we would rather say so than not.

How we decide a difference is real. One season is a small sample. A starter’s third-pass line rests on maybe a hundred plate appearances, and a hundred plate appearances of luck can look like a trend. So before we call a collapse real, we run a test statisticians would call significance and you can picture as replaying the season: rebuild a pitcher’s year two thousand times, each time drawing his actual starts at random — some starts counted twice, some left out — and recompute his decline in each replay. If his fall beats the league’s in at least 95 of every 100 replays, luck is a bad explanation and we print the number. If it doesn’t, we stay quiet about it. The ten pitchers named next all cleared that bar.

Which makes the exceptions worth naming. Ten starters this season decline by so much more than the league that we can say it with confidence — in at least 95 of every 100 replayed seasons, their drop from the second pass to the third is still bigger than the league’s. That is a statement about the size of their fall, not about where they end up: a couple of them are still better than average on the third pass, and falling further than anyone else to get there. The leaders are Cincinnati’s Nick Lodolo, the Athletics’ Jacob Lopez, Seattle’s Bryce Miller and Cleveland’s Slade Cecconi. Then, fifth-worst in baseball, the Cardinals’ Matthew Liberatore. His second pass in survival starts is a very good .648. From the third pass on in those same games it is 1.091. Shota Imanaga, Tarik Skubal, Taj Bradley, Christian Scott and Dylan Cease round out the ten.

And here is why the standard split can’t find them. Thirteen starters have three-way numbers that all look at or better than league average — the render every site shows you — and fail our survivorship screen anyway. Nine of the thirteen fail on the within-game step alone. Dylan Cease is the clearest. His line reads .489, .465, .693, against a league of .697, .739, .747 — every pass better than average, nothing on the card to flag. Now read the step instead of the levels. From the second time through to the third, hitters gain 229 points on him. The league gains 30.

And unlike Liberatore, nothing here is blended: Cease reached a third pass in all 24 of his starts, so his season line and his survival-start line are the same numbers. The split is telling the truth. It just invites you to compare him to the league instead of to himself. Cam Schlittler, Chris Sale, Reid Detmers and Gavin Williams show smaller versions of the same shape — clean-looking lines, a bigger step inside them — though none of the four clears the replay test’s bar for putting a number on it.

The mirror case is still Brady Singer, whose splits we picked apart last time. In the games he pitched into a third pass, hitters had him at .911 on the second pass and .714 from the third on. He is one of the very few starters who is genuinely better late, and his ugly aggregate second-pass number is the artifact rather than the third-pass one.

Why there’s no list of the ten who hold up best. We tried to build one — it is the piece this piece was supposed to be. A leaderboard of starters who run at or better than league average all three times through would be the natural flip side of the collapses above. It failed the replay test. Confidence at these sample sizes lives out at the extremes: the ten collapses are nameable because their falls clear the league by enormous margins. A “good three times through” screen is the opposite situation — by construction, its members sit right on the cut that defines it. In our drafted screen of nineteen, the median member kept his place in only 35 of 100 replayed seasons, and nobody kept it in even 80. Tightening the standard made it worse: eight members, and the most secure of them held on less than half the time. So when you see a leaderboard of TTO-proof starters somewhere else, ask how many of its names would survive a replayed season. Ours wouldn’t, and we would rather give you no list than that one.

The staff, in detail. St. Louis starters reach the third time through about as often as anyone — 115 of 132 starts, against a league rate of 84 percent. What happens next is the problem: the staff’s second pass in survival starts is .692 and its third pass is .799, a slide of 107 points against the league’s 30. Liberatore is most of that. Andre Pallante is the counterweight. He has reached a third pass in 23 of 24 starts, held hitters to .613 on the second pass in those games and .684 from the third on, and he holds up under the same replay test that catches Liberatore.

What changes in 700 Clark. Two things, both about restraint. First, when we quote a third-pass number we’ll keep showing you how many starts produced it and how the second pass looked in those same games — the practice we started last month. Second, and new this week: we now run every within-game difference through the replay test before we print it. At the sample sizes a single season gives you, most of these numbers point somewhere the data can’t confirm, so most of them will stop appearing. When you do see one, it cleared the bar. Liberatore’s clears it; Singer’s falls just short, which is why his improvement is described above instead of numbered. That’s not a limitation of the stat — it’s the size of the season.

HOW WE MEASURED IT

A companion to “The pitchers who fall off — and the ones who don’t.” Everything the piece claims is derived here, from raw counting stats, with the arithmetic shown. If you want to check us, this is the page to check.

What counts

Times through the order: for a batter facing a starting pitcher, his number on any given plate appearance is how many times he has now completed a plate appearance against that starter in that game. Appearances against relievers don’t advance the count.

A completed plate appearance is one that ends with a batter outcome: a hit, an out made at bat, a strikeout, a walk, a hit batsman, a sacrifice fly or bunt, or catcher’s interference. Plays that retire a runner while the batter stays at the plate are excluded — caught stealing, pickoffs, wild pitches, and the plays the data files label “Runner Out.” Across the 2026 season that exclusion covers 218 plays, against 84,776 completed plate appearances by starters.

A start belongs to the pitcher who throws the first play of his team’s defensive half. Starts facing fewer than nine batters are treated as openers; there were 176 league-wide.

A survival start is one where the starter faced some batter for a third time. Third and fourth passes are pooled throughout, since fourth passes are rare — 349 plate appearances in the whole league.

The formulas

For any group of plate appearances, add up the counting stats, then:

OBP = (hits + walks + hit-by-pitch) / (at-bats + walks + hit-by-pitch + sacrifice flies)
SLG = total bases / at-bats
OPS = OBP + SLG

Sacrifice bunts stay out of the on-base denominator, which is the standard convention.

Three derived measures carry the piece:

attainment      = starts reaching a third pass / starts with at least one completed PA

conditional gap = OPS on the 2nd pass in starts he did NOT survive
                  minus OPS on the 2nd pass in starts he did

within-game     = OPS on the 3rd pass and later, minus OPS on the 2nd pass,
change            both measured inside survival starts only

The last one is the piece’s central statistic. Both of its terms come from the same games, which is what makes it a fair comparison.

One floor governs everything. Any group under 50 plate appearances is reported as a count, never as a rate. A within-game change is withheld when either side falls under the floor, because the difference of two figures we won’t quote is itself not quotable.

Worked example: a full season

Liberatore made 25 starts and survived into a third pass in 22 of them. Pooling the plate appearances inside those 22:

Second pass — 198 PA, 181 AB, 46 H, 64 TB, 9 BB, 3 HBP, 4 SF

OBP = (46 + 9 + 3) / (181 + 9 + 3 + 4) = 58 / 197 = .294416
SLG = 64 / 181                                     = .353591
OPS                                                = .648008   ->  .648

Third pass and later, same 22 starts — 96 PA, 79 AB, 28 H, 50 TB, 16 BB, 0 HBP, 1 SF

OBP = (28 + 16 + 0) / (79 + 16 + 0 + 1) = 44 / 96 = .458333
SLG = 50 / 79                                     = .632911
OPS                                               = 1.091245  -> 1.091

within-game change = 1.091245 - .648008 = +.443237  -> +.443

Both groups clear the 50-PA floor, so both are quotable and so is the difference.

His season split says .757 on the second pass instead of .648. The gap is the three starts he didn’t finish: 22 plate appearances from early hooks, averaged in with the 198 from the games he survived. Blending is what hides the size of the fall.

The mirror case: when nothing is blended

Dylan Cease is worth stating carefully, because it is easy to get backwards. He reached a third pass in all 24 of his starts. He has no early-hook plate appearances at all. So for him:

season split, 2nd pass  = .4647 (225 PA)     2nd pass in survival starts = .4647 (225 PA)
season split, 3rd on    = .6934 (143 PA)     3rd on in survival starts   = .6934 (143 PA)

The two views are the same numbers, necessarily. Presenting the conditional figures as though they uncovered something his season line concealed would be wrong, because there is nothing concealed.

A different property of the same display is what catches him. His three passes read .489, .465 and .693 against a league of .697, .739 and .747 — every level better than average. The step between passes is the abnormal part:

Cease       .6934 - .4647 = +.2287   ->  229 points
the league  .7474 - .7175 = +.0299   ->   30 points

So the standard three-way split can fail a reader two ways. It can blend different kinds of game together, as it does with Liberatore, which conditioning fixes. Or it can be entirely accurate and still invite a comparison against the league rather than against the pitcher’s own earlier innings, as it does with Cease, which only reading the step fixes.

When a number is solid enough to print

A within-game change is the difference of two rates built on roughly 100 to 200 plate appearances each, so it carries both of their sampling errors. Before printing one, we ask whether it can be told apart from the league’s.

The test resamples a pitcher’s starts, with replacement, two thousand times. The start is the right unit because the manager’s decision to pull him operates on whole starts; resampling individual plate appearances would destroy the within-game pattern being measured. Each resample produces a fresh within-game change, and we count which side of the league figure it lands on:

for each of 2,000 resamples:
    draw a season of starts, with replacement, the same size as his real one
    compute the within-game change for that draw

share above the league figure, share below it
a number is quotable when the larger share reaches 95%

The test runs in both directions, since a pitcher who is clearly better late is as worth printing as one who collapses.

Reading the pitch-count rows correctly matters, and it cuts against us. Holding the pitch count roughly fixed trades one selection problem for another: inside a band, a second-pass plate appearance tends to come from a laboring outing while a third-pass one comes from an efficient outing. The band shows that the decline persists under the control. It does not separate batter familiarity from fatigue, and no version of this design can, because the two move together by construction.

What this does not settle

The within-game change records what happened. It does not explain why, and familiarity, fatigue and pitch count cannot be pulled apart here.

It also stops short of certifying anyone as good. A pass-or-fail screen at these sample sizes is unstable: on the same resampling test, the median member of a screen we drafted and rejected held its place in only 35% of replayed seasons. The piece names collapses, which sit far from any threshold, and ranks nobody as reliable.

There is one bias we should name before anyone else does, because it runs through the central statistic. Managers pull starters partly because they believe in the third-time penalty, and the within-game change is computed only over the starts they chose to extend. Pull earlier and the third passes that still happen come from the best days only, which would make the decline look smaller than it is. So the figure partly reflects how a league uses its starters, not only how starters pitch. Two weak signals point that way — across the 30 teams, those who let starters go deeper show a slightly larger decline, and from 2025 to 2026 attainment and the decline fell together — but neither is close to strong enough to lean on, and untangling it needs a multi-season series we have not built yet. It is the next thing we are looking at.

Year-over-year comparisons need two separate margins kept apart, and it is easy to quote the wrong one. We checked our 2026 data against an independent source for the 2025 season and the two agreed to about a thousandth, so a difference between seasons is not an artifact of which source we used. That is a statement about the instrument. The binding limit is the sample: resampling the league’s within-game change puts its own standard error near thirteen thousandths, roughly twenty times coarser. The 2025-to-2026 move of nineteen thousandths is therefore under one and a half standard errors — a difference we can describe, not a change we can claim.

And the season is partial: 1,964 games of roughly 2,430, weighted toward April.
